The answer in one page

Losses come from how the network is designed, not mainly from vendor prices. Sites were priced without knowing their real cost, spread across too many small vendors, cleaned on habit-based schedules, and paid whether or not the work was verified. The fix is a density-first operating model run on one per-site profit view.

    How the prototype works

    A synthetic world

    2,000 sites around real U.S. metros and small towns, six national accounts, about 180 vendors, contracts, invoices and Peazy-style visit data. The same code can later run on the company's real data.

    Problems planted at known sites

    Fourteen kinds of problem, from legacy prices to crews that keep quitting. The finder sees only bills, contracts and visit signals, never the answer key, so we can score it.

    Fixes tested before they touch anyone

    Ten levers re-price all 2,000 sites through the same profit model, with guardrails for quality, crews and customers, and a randomized pilot to prove cause and effect.

    Walk through the six questions

    Q1 · Your problem framing and assumptions

    Frame the problem

    Which locations lose money, why, and what operating model makes the whole portfolio profitable without hurting cleaning quality, crews or customers?

    The unit of analysis is one site in one month: what it earns, what it costs, and why the two differ.

    M1 · gross spread

    Customer revenue minus vendor charges.

    M2 · contribution (used here)

    M1 minus re-cleans, credits, supplies and inspection time. A site that is negative on M2 loses money every month it is served.

    M3 · fully loaded

    M2 minus account management, billing, platform cost and the cost of paying vendors before customers pay us.

    Two reference buildings

    The same 10,000 sq ft office, cleaned 5 nights a week. One is healthy. Its twin was priced years ago, sits 53 night drive-minutes from any other site, and needs re-cleans. Illustrative numbers.

    Q3 · How you would identify root causes

    Find the root causes

    Do not stop at "this site loses money". For every site, compare what it should cost with what it actually costs, and assign the gap to causes using bills, contracts and Peazy signals.

    Each losing site gets one or more cause codes, each with a dollar amount, so the fix matches the cause.

    1. Clean the dataFlag missing sq ft; re-scale lumped invoices to scheduled visits.
    2. Should-costSq ft ÷ production rate × labor rate × visits, plus supplies and inspections. Travel is tagged separately by density tier.
    3. Variance waterfallSplit the gap: price, travel, minimums, extra hours, quality, supplies.
    4. Read the signalsDwell time, photos, scores, tickets, occupancy and access notes tell similar causes apart.
    5. Score itPrecision and recall against the planted causes, then a stress test with double the noise.

    Q4 · Your solution and the role of technology

    Fix the model, not just the price

    Ten levers in three groups: a density-first operating model, verified and fair vendor pay, and pricing the real work. Switch them on and off; the same profit model re-prices all 2,000 sites.

    People and legal guardrails

    Vendors employ and direct their own crews. Routes are suggestions their dispatchers own; the platform records geofence events only at the site boundary, never continuous location; completion photos show rooms, not people; and the model goes through a legal review for joint-employer risk before rollout.

    Q6 · What you would build or test first

    Build the Lens, then run a fair pilot

    Do not change 2,000 sites at once. First build the Location Profitability Lens (Q2 and Q3 of this prototype, on real data). Then test the operational levers in randomly chosen zones and compare them with zones left alone.

    Random assignment is what lets us say the change caused the result.

    Build first · weeks 2-6

    Location Profitability Lens

    Does: joins Peazy visits, contracts and vendor invoice lines into one P&L row per site per month; computes should-cost; tags root-cause codes with dollars; clusters sites; raises margin alerts.

    Stack: abrightlab's own: a daily Lambda job in TypeScript writing to Postgres on RDS through Prisma, a NestJS GraphQL API, and a Next.js dashboard like this one. An LLM reads contracts and lumped invoices, with confidence scores.

    Timeline: after a two-week data audit, about four weeks if invoices already carry location IDs; longer if lumped invoices must be parsed first.

    Test first · 90 days

    Zone-randomized pilot

    Design: the 40 cluster zones with the most booked loss, randomly split 20 treatment and 20 control. Control zones get no network-wide changes until the readout.

    Levers tested: the operational levers selected in Q4. Cluster contracts, routes and pricing need new contracts or renewals, and schedule changes need customer agreement, so they come after the pilot.

    Decision rule: scale if the lower end of the 95% interval is above +1 point of M2 margin and every guardrail holds in the treated zones.

    Pilot readout: change in zone M2 margin after 90 days

    About this prototype

    Built by Bhavani for the abrightlab 2,000-Location Challenge.

    My idea

    A synthetic world with planted problems

    Instead of guessing from the outside, build a copy of the network, plant the problems the brief describes, and prove the tools find them. The same code can then run on the real data.

    My idea

    A two-way on-time promise

    Inspired by a pizza-delivery guarantee, made fair both ways for business customers cleaned at night. Every late or missed visit gets a reason code from geofence evidence. Vendor-caused misses give the customer an automatic credit, paid by the vendor. Customer-caused misses (wrong address, no access) are shown with proof and stay billable under a clause agreed in the contract. A site passport collected at sign-up prevents most of them, and crews stop being blamed for problems they did not cause.

    What is real and what is made up

    Everything in the world is synthetic: the sites, customers, vendors and every dollar figure. Vendor names are invented to make the examples easier to follow; any match with a real company is a coincidence. The real-world anchors are the metro and town locations, the U.S. state map (Census Bureau boundaries via us-atlas), a cleaner wage near the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics median for janitors (about $17 an hour), and routine office production rates of roughly 2,500-4,000 sq ft per hour in line with industry cleaning-time guides. All results are illustrative and should be recalibrated on the company's data.

    How it is built

    Plain JavaScript, no framework. sim.js holds the world generator, the profit model, the root-cause finder, DBSCAN clustering, route planning, the levers, the vendor scorecard and the pilot. app.js draws the page. The finder reads only observable data, and a test proves it never touches the answer key. The architecture folder holds the production data model and API schema, and ml holds the machine-learning comparison (Python, scikit-learn). A GitHub Actions pipeline runs the tests, validates both schemas and re-runs the comparison and opens every page in a test browser on every push.

    AI tools used

    Claude Code (Anthropic) was used to research janitorial cost structures, draft the analysis, and write and test the simulation code and this page. The two core ideas above are mine, and I directed and reviewed the build.
